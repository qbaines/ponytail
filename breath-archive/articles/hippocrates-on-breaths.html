What “On Breaths” tells us about ancient Greek medicine

Historical essay, not health advice. Ancient medical theories are discussed as history, not as descriptions of modern anatomy or treatment.

“On Breaths” (De flatibus) is a short work transmitted in the Hippocratic corpus, the collection of medical writings associated with Hippocrates and his school. The corpus was written by multiple authors over time; calling a work “Hippocratic” does not prove that the historical Hippocrates personally wrote it.

In Francis Adams’s nineteenth-century translation, “On Breaths” discusses air and vapors as causes of illness. This belongs to an older explanatory model, not modern respiratory physiology. The translation is useful as a historical artifact and a starting point for reading, but its date and vocabulary matter: old English renderings of Greek medical terms can obscure uncertainty or disagreement.

Read the theory in its own setting

Ancient writers reasoned about air and breath using the medical concepts available to them. That makes the treatise valuable evidence about how some Greek medical authors tried to explain bodily processes—not a reliable account of lungs, oxygen, or disease by present-day standards. A modern reader should resist translating every use of “air,” “wind,” or “breath” directly into a modern physiological mechanism.

There is a further limit: this text is about explanation, not necessarily a practical breathing regimen. A discussion of breath in an old medical work should not be turned into a modern self-help method unless the source, context, and evidence actually support that use.

How to use an archived edition

Start by recording author attribution as uncertain, translator, edition, and publication date. Then follow the translation’s notes and compare it with a scholarly edition if a particular claim matters. Quote sparingly, verify wording in the scan, and link readers to the full source so they can check the context themselves.

This is a different kind of historical evidence from a yoga practice manual: the source belongs to a distinct language, culture, and medical tradition. Similar words do not prove that Greek and South Asian theories of breath meant the same thing or influenced one another.
